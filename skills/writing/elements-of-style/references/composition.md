# Composition (Strunk 1920, rules 8–18)

Open [strunk-1920.md](strunk-1920.md) only if an example is needed.

## 8. One paragraph, one topic

A short subject may be one paragraph. Otherwise, each paragraph is one
step of the argument. Do not start a new paragraph on every sentence.

## 9. Topic sentence; end in conformity

For expository paragraphs: state the topic early, develop it, land where
you aimed. Narrative and `writing-prose` beats may skip a formal topic
sentence.

## 10. Active voice (usually)

`I shall always remember my first visit` beats `My first visit will always
be remembered by me`.

Keep passive when the receiver is the paragraph’s topic. Avoid stacked
passives and hollow nominalizations:

- `A survey of this region was made` → `This region was surveyed`
- `There were dead leaves lying on the ground` → `Dead leaves covered the ground`

## 11. Positive form

`He usually came late` beats `He was not very often on time`.
`forgot` beats `did not remember`. `ignored` beats `did not pay attention to`.

Use *not* for denial or antithesis, not for evasion.
`Not charity, but simple justice` is strong.

## 12. Definite, specific, concrete

`It rained every day for a week` beats `A period of unfavorable weather set in`.
`He grinned as he pocketed the coin` beats `He showed satisfaction as he took
possession of his well-earned reward`.

General claims still need a particular instance. This is also Spencer’s
economy-of-attention rule (quoted by Strunk).

## 13. Omit needless words

A sentence should contain no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary
sentences — not that every sentence must be short, but that every word tells.

| Cut | Keep |
|-----|------|
| the question as to whether | whether |
| there is no doubt but that | no doubt / doubtless |
| used for fuel purposes | used for fuel |
| he is a man who | he |
| in a hasty manner | hastily |
| this is a subject which | this subject |
| owing to the fact that | since / because |
| in spite of the fact that | though / although |
| I was unaware of the fact that | I did not know |
| who is / which was (appositive) | the noun alone |

Combine a chain of thin sentences that are one idea.

## 14. Don’t string loose *and/but/so* sentences

A whole paragraph of `X, and Y. A, while B. C, and D.` is sing-song.
Recast some as simple sentences, semicolon pairs, or periodic sentences
so the structure matches the thought.

Do **not** use this rule to flatten `writing-prose` burstiness. Variety is
the point.

## 15. Parallel form for parallel ideas

Similar content → similar syntax. Don’t vary form just to “sound fresh.”

`to swim, to bike, and running` → `to swim, to bike, and to run`.

## 16. Keep related words together

Subject near verb, verb near object, modifier next to what it modifies.
Don’t park a long aside between them.

## 17. One tense in a summary

Summaries of plot, papers, or systems stay in one tense. Don’t drift
present → past → present without a reason.

## 18. Emphasis at the end

The stress position is the end of the sentence (and of the paragraph).
Put new or important matter last; put familiar matter first.
