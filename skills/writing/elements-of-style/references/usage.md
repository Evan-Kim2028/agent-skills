# Usage (Strunk 1920, rules 1–7)

Open [strunk-1920.md](strunk-1920.md) only if an example is needed.

## 1. Possessive singular: add `'s`

`Charles's friend`, `Burns's poems`. No apostrophe in `hers`, `its`, `theirs`,
`yours`, `oneself`. Ancient names in *-es/-is* may take the *of* form
(`the laws of Moses`).

## 2. Series comma

`red, white, and blue`. Comma after each term except the last. Exception:
some firm names (`Brown, Shipley & Co.`).

## 3. Parenthetic commas come in pairs

Never one comma of a pair. Non-restrictive clauses take commas; restrictive
clauses do not.

- Non-restrictive: `The audience, which had at first been indifferent, became interested.`
- Restrictive: `The candidate who best meets these requirements will obtain the place.`

Dates: `April 6, 1917`. Do not comma-splice independent clauses (rule 5).

## 4. Comma before a coordinating conjunction

`The records have disappeared, and the story can no longer be reconstructed.`

If the second clause starts with an adverb (`therefore`, `accordingly`), use
a semicolon, not a comma. Same subject + close `and`: omit the comma
(`He has experience and is competent`). Same subject + `but`: keep the comma.

## 5. No comma splice

Two independent clauses, no conjunction → semicolon or two sentences.

`It is nearly half past five; we cannot reach town before dark.`

Short, parallel clauses may take commas: `Man proposes, God disposes.`

## 6. No accidental fragments

Do not use a period where a comma belongs.

`I met them on a liner. Coming home from Liverpool.` → one sentence.

**House override:** an emphatic one-word sentence is allowed when the stress
is real (`No reply.`). `writing-prose` punches stay if they are intentional.

## 7. Opening modifier refers to the subject

`Walking down the road, he saw a woman` — *walking* is *he*.

Fix danglers by naming the actor:

- `On arriving in Chicago, his friends met him.`
- `When he arrived in Chicago, his friends met him.`
